Key facts
  1. Nvidia's board accepted Huang's resignation as president, CEO and board member on Nov. 11; it was announced on Nov. 12, effective immediately.[1][2]
  2. Colette Kress, Nvidia's chief financial officer since 2013, is interim CEO while the board searches inside and outside the company for a permanent successor. She keeps the CFO job in the meantime.[1][2]
  3. Harvey C. Jones, a director since Nvidia's founding year of 1993, became the company's first-ever board chair.[1][2]
  4. Huang co-founded Nvidia in 1993 and had been its only CEO.[2][9]
  5. Nvidia shares closed down about 4 percent on the day of the announcement. The company's market value had already fallen from about $5.78 trillion on Oct. 6 to about $4.51 trillion on Nov. 6.[3][6][8]
  6. Nvidia reports its quarterly results on Nov. 17, and House committee chairs have asked Huang to a closed-door briefing on Nov. 18.[2][7]
What happened

In late October, The New York Times reported that U.S. intelligence agencies had for about a year been watching Alice Huang, a Chinese executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore company that buys Nvidia chips, over her ties to China, and that Jensen Huang had a close relationship with her. The two are not related. According to the reporting, officials did not believe Jensen Huang knew she might be gathering information, and neither of them has been charged. China has called the spying allegations groundless.

Nvidia's board first opened an independent investigation. Huang then admitted being unfaithful to his wife and apologised to his family and employees. Further reports described a relationship with a senior Nvidia executive who reported directly to him. On Nov. 12 the company announced that he had resigned. Nvidia did not give a reason; the board's statement praised his contributions and said operations, customer commitments and product plans would continue without interruption.

Why it matters beyond Nvidia

Nvidia's graphics processors are the main engine behind the current wave of AI, from chatbots to scientific research, which is why it grew into one of the most valuable companies in history. Huang was unusually central to that story: he co-founded the company, ran it for more than three decades and was its public face.

The episode also lands in the middle of a sensitive policy fight. Washington restricts sales of Nvidia's most advanced chips to China, and U.S. officials have been examining whether companies such as Megaspeed helped Chinese buyers get around those limits. Lawmakers now want to know whether national security was put at risk.

What's still uncertain

Who will lead Nvidia permanently, and whether that person will come from inside the company, is open. So is the outcome of the congressional inquiry: Representative Brian Mast has said Huang indicated he is willing to appear, while noting that the resignation changes the situation. It is also not yet known whether any sensitive information actually reached anyone it should not have; reporting so far has not established that.

Views on the business impact differ. Analysts describe a rare key-person risk for a company so closely tied to its founder, and some see the moment as a test for the AI boom as a whole; Nvidia's board says it has full confidence in Kress and the leadership team.

What to watch next

Nvidia's quarterly results on Nov. 17, its first under interim leadership; the closed-door House briefing requested for Nov. 18; the board's CEO search; and any change in U.S. rules on chip exports to China.
