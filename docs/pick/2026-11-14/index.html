Congo's Ebola outbreak passes 11,000 cases — and there is still no proven vaccine for this strain

The second-largest Ebola outbreak ever recorded is still growing in eastern Congo. Scientists are racing to test vaccines while conflict and hunger make the response harder.

AI-written report · based on 10 sources · reviewed 3 times · approved by the editor

Why we picked it. It is the largest outbreak in Congo's history and the second-largest Ebola epidemic on record. It is still spreading, it is caused by a type of Ebola with no approved vaccine or treatment, and it is colliding with a hunger crisis that affects tens of millions of people. What happens next will shape how the world prepares for the next outbreak.

Key facts

  1. As of Nov. 11, Congo's health ministry had recorded 11,042 cases and 5,387 deaths — nearly half of everyone infected.[1][3]
  2. Before this year, Congo's worst outbreak (2018–2020) had about 3,500 cases. Only the 2014–2016 West Africa epidemic, with about 28,000 infections, was larger.[2][3]
  3. The outbreak was declared in mid-May in Ituri province and has reached eight of Congo's 26 provinces. Cases have also been reported in Uganda, Kenya and Nigeria.[1][2][3]
  4. It is caused by the Bundibugyo virus. The approved Ebola vaccines and antibody treatments were built for a different type, the Zaire virus.[2][5]
  5. Two vaccines designed specifically for Bundibugyo — from Oxford with the Serum Institute of India, and from Moderna — are joining a trial in Congo. Results are expected in early 2027.[5][6]
  6. About 28.8 million people in Congo face acute food insecurity through December, according to the UN-backed IPC analysis.[7]

What happened

Congo declared this Ebola outbreak in mid-May in the northeastern province of Ituri. Six months later, official figures show more than 11,000 infections and close to 5,400 deaths, making it far larger than any earlier outbreak in the country.

Ituri still accounts for roughly 70 percent of all cases, but Congo's health minister says North Kivu province is now where most new infections are happening. Renewed fighting in both provinces, attacks on health workers, poor roads and mistrust fuelled by misinformation have all slowed the response. Schools in Ituri have lost teachers to the disease.

Ebola spreads through contact with the bodily fluids of a sick person or someone who has died of the disease. It is not airborne.

Why this outbreak is so hard to stop

Most past outbreaks were caused by the Zaire type of Ebola, for which there is a licensed vaccine, Ervebo. This one is caused by Bundibugyo, a rarer type with no approved vaccine or treatment.

Researchers are already testing whether Ervebo offers any cross-protection, in a trial called Tumaini that vaccinates the contacts of confirmed cases in rings around each infection. In early November, WHO advisers recommended adding two vaccines designed for Bundibugyo itself — the first time such vaccines will be tested during an outbreak. Both use technology proven during the COVID-19 pandemic.

The outbreak is also hitting a population already under strain. Conflict and displacement have left about 28.8 million people in Congo facing acute food insecurity, and the UN's food agency warns that hungry families who cannot afford to stay home are less able to follow isolation and quarantine advice.

What it means for you

For people in Canada the risk remains low. The Government of Canada advises avoiding non-essential travel to the Democratic Republic of the Congo (level 3 notice), and border measures apply to travellers who have been in Congo, Uganda or South Sudan in the previous 21 days. Officials stress that Ebola is not spread through the air and that the risk for most travellers is low if precautions are followed.

If you have been in the affected region and feel unwell within 21 days of leaving, contact a health care provider and tell them about your travel.

What's still uncertain

No one yet knows whether any of the vaccines in the trial will work against Bundibugyo — being included in a trial is not proof that a vaccine protects. Early results from treatment studies are expected in the coming months, and vaccine results not until early 2027.

Experts also disagree about border measures. WHO advises against travel and trade restrictions for this outbreak, while Canada, the United States and Mexico have aligned stricter entry rules. Some Canadian infectious-disease specialists have called the broad measures unsupported by the science and an obstacle to sending help, while others consider them reasonable precautions.

What to watch next

Whether new cases in North Kivu begin to fall, whether the outbreak reaches more provinces or countries, the first treatment-trial results, and whether funding for both the Ebola response and food aid keeps pace with needs that the IPC expects to grow into 2027.
